The step between a promising email exchange and a first commercial order from an Indian herbal ingredient supplier is almost always a sample. It is also the step most likely to go wrong in small, avoidable ways - an unclear cost split, a sample that does not match what the buyer actually needs to evaluate, or a customs hold that turns a five-day courier estimate into three weeks with no communication in between.

This guide walks through how the sample request process actually works: what to ask for, what it costs, realistic timelines, and what to verify before treating a sample result as a green light for a bulk order.

---

## What to Request - and Why the Sample Itself Needs a Specification

A sample request is not simply "send me a sample of ashwagandha extract." It needs the same specification precision as a full RFQ, because a sample that does not match the intended commercial specification tells the buyer nothing useful. At minimum, a sample request should specify:

- **The exact form and standardization** - the same withanolide, curcuminoid, or boswellic acid percentage (or equivalent marker) the buyer intends to order in bulk, not whatever the supplier has readily on hand
- **Sample quantity** - typically 100 grams to 500 grams is sufficient for internal quality evaluation, sensory assessment, or formulation trials; larger quantities (1kg or more) are occasionally needed for pilot-scale manufacturing trials and should be requested explicitly
- **A batch-specific Certificate of Analysis**, requested before the sample ships - this confirms the sample corresponds to a real, tested production lot rather than an unrepresentative or hand-selected parcel
- **Packaging intent** - whether the sample needs to reflect final export packaging (relevant if evaluating moisture protection or shelf stability) or is purely for content evaluation, where standard courier-safe packaging is sufficient

Requesting a sample without specifying standardization level is the most common reason a sample evaluation does not translate cleanly into a bulk order: the buyer evaluates one specification and the supplier later quotes bulk pricing against a different one.

## Who Pays, and How Much

Sample cost conventions are fairly consistent across Indian herbal ingredient suppliers:

**The ingredient itself** - up to roughly 500 grams - is typically provided free of charge or for a nominal handling fee by most established suppliers, since a sample request from a genuine buyer is treated as a normal part of the sales process.

**International courier cost is almost always the buyer's responsibility.** For a small parcel, this typically runs somewhere in the range of 30 to 80 US dollars depending on destination country and courier service selected, and is usually invoiced directly by the supplier or charged to the buyer's own courier account if one is provided.

**Larger sample quantities carry additional cost.** Requests above roughly 1kg - relevant for pilot manufacturing trials rather than lab evaluation - may incur a per-kilogram charge from the supplier, particularly for high-value standardized extracts or essential oils where even a 1kg sample represents meaningful raw material cost.

Buyers should clarify the cost split explicitly before the sample ships, rather than assuming the full cost is free - a supplier who has not clarified this upfront will typically raise it when invoicing the courier charge, which is a minor friction point best avoided.

## Realistic Timelines

**Stock samples:** Where the supplier already holds the requested specification in inventory, dispatch typically happens within **2 to 5 business days** of the request being confirmed. International courier transit adds a further **3 to 7 business days** depending on destination, giving a total realistic window of roughly one to two weeks from request to delivery.

**Non-stock samples:** Where the requested specification requires a small dedicated pull from a production run, additional testing, or a batch that is not currently in inventory, total lead time can extend to **2 to 3 weeks**. This is common for less commonly requested standardization levels or for ingredients with seasonal raw material availability.

Buyers should always request a confirmed dispatch date rather than assuming stock availability, and should treat an open-ended "we will send it soon" response as a signal to ask directly whether the specification requested is in stock or requires a fresh pull.

## Customs Considerations for Sample Shipments

Sample quantities of plant-derived material are not automatically exempt from import scrutiny. Even parcels declared as low-value or no-commercial-value samples can be held for inspection at destination customs, particularly in markets with strict phytosanitary controls on plant material.

Two practices reduce the likelihood of a hold:

**Accurate product description on the courier waybill.** A vague or generic description increases the chance of manual inspection; a specific, accurate description (for example, "botanical extract powder, ashwagandha root, for laboratory evaluation, not for resale") is processed more predictably.

**A realistic declared value.** Declaring an artificially low value to reduce potential duty exposure on a sample can itself trigger a customs query, since customs officers are familiar with this practice. A modest, defensible declared value for a small sample quantity is processed more smoothly than either an unrealistically low figure or an unexplained high one.

Buyers importing into markets with known restrictions on plant-derived material - certain botanical categories in the EU and Japan in particular - should confirm with their own customs broker whether any import permit or notification is required even at sample quantities, rather than assuming sample size automatically falls below any regulatory threshold.

## From Sample to Bulk: What to Verify Before Scaling

A satisfactory sample result is necessary but not sufficient to place a bulk order with confidence. Before scaling, confirm:

**The bulk batch will match the sample specification.** Some suppliers provide a sample drawn from a higher grade or more tightly controlled batch than their standard commercial output. Confirm explicitly that the bulk order will be produced - or drawn from stock - at the same specification evaluated in the sample, not a nominally similar but distinct grade.

**The Certificate of Analysis for the sample lot is retained for comparison.** When the bulk shipment arrives, compare its Certificate of Analysis against the sample's to confirm consistency. A significant divergence between the two is worth raising with the supplier before accepting the shipment.

**Pricing, MOQ, and lead time are reconfirmed for the bulk quantity.** Sample-stage conversations about price and availability do not always carry over automatically to a bulk order - reconfirm these terms explicitly once the sample is approved, rather than assuming the informal figures discussed during sampling still apply.

**Testing scope for the bulk shipment matches destination market requirements**, which may be broader than what was checked on the sample - a sample evaluated for identity and standardization alone does not confirm that a full pesticide residue or heavy metals panel appropriate to your destination market will pass on the bulk batch.

## Summary

A sample request from an Indian herbal ingredient supplier should specify the same standardization and form intended for bulk order, request a batch-specific Certificate of Analysis before dispatch, and set clear expectations on cost split and timeline upfront. Realistic costs run to a nominal or no ingredient charge plus 30 to 80 US dollars in courier fees, with delivery in one to two weeks for stock items and up to three weeks for non-stock specifications. A positive sample result is the start of due diligence, not the end of it - confirming that a bulk order will match the sample specification, with a reconfirmed price, MOQ, and testing scope, is what actually determines whether the sample process leads to a reliable commercial relationship.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does a herbal ingredient sample from India cost?**

Most suppliers provide a 100 gram to 500 gram sample free or for a nominal fee, but the buyer typically covers courier cost, usually 30 to 80 US dollars depending on destination. Larger samples above roughly 1kg may carry an additional charge.

**How long does it take to receive a sample from an Indian supplier?**

Stock items typically dispatch within 2 to 5 business days with 3 to 7 business days transit. Non-stock specifications requiring a fresh batch pull or testing can take 2 to 3 weeks total.

**Should I request a Certificate of Analysis with the sample?**

Yes, always, requested before the sample ships. It confirms the sample corresponds to a real tested production lot and provides a baseline for comparison against independent testing or the eventual bulk shipment.

**Can sample couriers be held up at customs?**

Yes, particularly for plant-derived material in markets with strict phytosanitary controls. An accurate product description and a realistic declared value on the waybill reduce the likelihood of a hold.

**What should I check before moving from a sample to a bulk order?**

Confirm the bulk batch matches the sample specification, retain the sample's Certificate of Analysis for comparison against the bulk shipment, and reconfirm pricing, MOQ, lead time, and destination-market testing scope before placing the order.
