Lakadong turmeric's reputation for high curcumin content is one of the main reasons GCC buyers seek it out, and it is also the single easiest claim for a supplier to overstate. This guide sets out exactly what to ask for, and why the distinction between "this variety is known for high curcumin" and "this specific batch tested at X% curcumin" is the difference between marketing and a verifiable specification.

**Quick answer:** GCC buyers should ask for a batch-specific Certificate of Analysis showing curcumin content tested via HPLC (not colorimetric or titration methods), referencing the exact lot number of the shipment being purchased, issued or verified by an accredited independent laboratory - and should treat any curcumin figure not tied to that specific batch as a variety-level reputation, not a guaranteed spec.

---

## Why Does This Distinction Matter So Much for Lakadong Turmeric?

Government and trade sourcing literature commonly places Lakadong turmeric's curcumin content in the 6-10% range, with some commercial suppliers advertising figures of 9-10% or higher in their marketing material. That range is real, well-documented at the variety level, and part of why Lakadong turmeric commands a premium price over conventional Indian turmeric, which typically tests closer to 2-5%. But a variety-level range describes the crop's general reputation across many harvests, farms, and years - not the specific bag or container a buyer is being asked to pay for today.

Curcumin content is not a fixed trait like a botanical name. It moves with harvest timing, seasonal rainfall and soil conditions, how the rhizomes were dried, how long the material has been stored, and how it was processed into powder if applicable. Two batches from the same growing region, harvested in different years or processed differently, can show meaningfully different curcumin results. Treating the variety's reputation as an assumed spec for a specific shipment is where buyers get exposed - either paying a premium price for material that does not test at the premium level, or building a downstream product formulation around a number that the actual batch cannot support.

## Why Marketing Inflation Is a Real Risk Here

Because curcumin percentage is the primary commercial differentiator buyers use to justify Lakadong turmeric's price premium, there is a direct financial incentive for suppliers to quote figures at the high end of the known range, or above it, without batch-specific evidence behind the number. This is not necessarily deliberate misrepresentation in every case - a supplier may genuinely believe their product tests in that range based on historical batches or informal testing - but from a buyer's perspective, an unverified figure carries the same risk regardless of whether the overstatement is intentional or simply unverified. The only defence is requiring the number to be demonstrated on the specific batch being sold, every time, rather than accepted as a standing claim about the supplier's product line.

## What Is HPLC Testing, and Why Does the Test Method Matter?

High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) separates and quantifies the individual curcuminoid compounds in a sample with high specificity, and it is the test method generally accepted as accurate for curcumin content determination in regulated food, nutraceutical, and pharmaceutical markets. Older or lower-cost alternative methods - colorimetric assays or titration-based methods in particular - are less specific: they can respond to other compounds present in the sample alongside curcumin, and in practice tend to report higher curcumin percentages than HPLC testing on the same material.

This matters directly for Lakadong turmeric because a supplier under commercial pressure to substantiate a high curcumin claim has an incentive to use, or to cite, a less specific test method that produces a more favourable number. A Certificate of Analysis that states a curcumin percentage without naming the test method should prompt an immediate, specific question: was this tested via HPLC, and if not, what method was used and why. A supplier confident in their result will generally answer this without hesitation.

## What Should a Lot-Specific Certificate of Analysis Actually Contain?

For curcumin content specifically, a usable Certificate of Analysis should show:

1. **The curcumin percentage result** - a specific figure, not a range presented as a guarantee.
2. **The test method** - explicitly named as HPLC.
3. **The batch or lot number** - matching the physical shipment and its packaging labels, not a generic product code disconnected from the specific goods.
4. **Testing laboratory name and accreditation status** - ideally referencing ISO 17025 accreditation or an equivalent recognised standard.
5. **Date of testing** - a result more than 12 months old should prompt a request for retesting, since curcumin content in stored material can degrade over time.

A Certificate of Analysis missing the batch number is effectively a marketing data sheet. It may accurately describe the supplier's typical product, but it provides no evidence about the specific shipment being offered - which is the entire point of requiring one.

## In-House Testing vs Independent Laboratory Verification

Suppliers commonly provide an in-house Certificate of Analysis generated by their own quality control laboratory. This is a reasonable starting point for lower-risk, non-regulated applications, provided the in-house lab holds recognised accreditation and the buyer has some basis for trusting its results.

For nutraceutical, pharmaceutical, or other regulated end-uses - and for any first order from a new supplier regardless of end-use - commissioning an independent, accredited third-party laboratory to test the sample or an early batch is the more defensible approach. Independent testing removes the structural conflict of interest in a supplier testing their own material and reporting the result that supports their asking price. Once a supplier's in-house results have been cross-checked against independent testing on two or three occasions and found reliable, buyers can reasonably reduce - though rarely eliminate entirely - how often they commission independent re-testing on subsequent orders.

## What Should a Buyer Ask For, Specifically?

A precise request to a supplier should sound like this: "Please provide a Certificate of Analysis for lot number [X], showing curcumin content tested via HPLC, issued by [named laboratory], including that laboratory's accreditation reference and the date of testing." This framing does three things a vaguer request does not: it names the batch, names the required method, and asks for the credentials that let the buyer judge whether to trust the result without independent verification.

Buyers should be specifically cautious of responses that offer a curcumin percentage with no method named, that cite "our lab" without accreditation information, or that provide a CoA referencing a different or unspecified lot number than the one being shipped.

**Why shouldn't I rely on a supplier's stated curcumin percentage?**

A supplier's stated percentage is often a variety-level or marketing figure rather than a batch-specific lab result, and even where it reflects a genuine past test, curcumin content varies from harvest to harvest and batch to batch due to soil conditions, rainfall, harvest timing, and post-harvest processing. Relying on a stated figure without a matching batch-specific Certificate of Analysis means you have no verification that the shipment you actually receive meets that number.

**What is the difference between HPLC testing and other curcumin test methods?**

Older or lower-cost methods such as colorimetric assays or titration are less specific - they can be influenced by other compounds in the sample and tend to overstate curcumin content compared to HPLC. Buyers should confirm the test method named on any Certificate of Analysis is HPLC, not a colorimetric or titration-based result presented without that distinction being made clear.

**What exactly should a Certificate of Analysis show for curcumin content?**
