When international buyers invest in supplier qualification - auditing facilities, testing samples, reviewing certifications - the instinct is to treat that due diligence as a one-time event. The supplier passed. The relationship is established. Now purchasing begins.

This framing misses the most important phase of supply risk management: the ongoing contractual framework that governs what the supplier must do after the first order. A supplier qualification is a point-in-time assessment. A quality agreement is the mechanism that holds that standard across every subsequent batch, year after year, as production staff turn over, raw material sources shift, and the commercial relationship loses the attentiveness of a first order.

The audit and qualification posts in this series cover pre-order diligence. This post covers what comes after: the quality agreement - what it is, why it is distinct from a purchase contract, and what it must contain to be genuinely protective in the Indian herbal ingredient supply context.

---

## What a Quality Agreement Is and Is Not

A quality agreement is a technical and quality framework document that defines the standards a supplier must meet across an ongoing supply relationship. It is not a purchase contract. It is not a service agreement. It is not an NDA, although it typically contains confidentiality provisions.

A purchase contract defines the commercial terms of a transaction: price per kilogram, payment terms, minimum order quantity, delivery Incoterm, and the legal remedy if goods do not arrive. A quality agreement defines the technical and procedural standards that govern how the product must be manufactured, tested, documented, and delivered - standards that apply to every purchase order placed under the commercial relationship, regardless of the price, the volume, or the batch number.

The distinction matters in practice because the two documents govern different failure modes. A purchase contract protects you when a supplier ships at the wrong price or fails to deliver on time. A quality agreement protects you when a supplier changes their raw material source without telling you, switches extraction methods, loses a certification, or fails to notify you of a batch that narrowly passed their internal testing but would not pass yours.

In regulated markets - EU novel food, US DSHEA, Japan health claim registration - the quality agreement is also an audit trail document. A regulatory inspector reviewing your ingredient supply chain wants to see that your specification is locked, that your supplier's documentation obligations are defined, and that you have a documented process for handling non-conformance. A quality agreement provides all three.

## Product Specification Lock

The most important element of a quality agreement is the product specification, and the most important characteristic of that specification is that it is fixed by mutual agreement and cannot be unilaterally changed.

A product specification lock for an Indian herbal ingredient should define the following:

**Identity.** Botanical name (genus, species, and authority), plant part used (root, leaf, seed, bark), and product form (whole powder, spray-dried extract, liquid extract). For standardised extracts, the extraction solvent or solvent system used.

**Active marker compound.** The target concentration range for the compound being standardised - for example, 5 percent withanolides for ashwagandha root extract, or 95 percent curcuminoids for turmeric extract - with the specific test method used to measure it (HPLC, UV spectrophotometry, AOAC method number). The test method matters as much as the target: two different HPLC methods can produce materially different results for the same sample.

**Physical parameters.** Particle size (mesh size or median particle diameter), moisture content maximum, bulk density, and colour description where relevant to identifying out-of-specification product on receipt.

**Microbiological limits.** Total aerobic plate count, total yeast and mould count, and specific pathogen absence requirements - E. coli, Salmonella, Staphylococcus aureus - to the limits applicable in the destination market.

**Heavy metal limits.** Lead, arsenic, cadmium, and mercury, expressed in parts per million, to the limits applicable in the destination market. EU limits, Prop 65 limits, and Japan Ministry of Health limits are not identical - specify which applies.

**Pesticide residue standard.** The applicable regulatory standard - EU MRL, USDA Organic program requirements, Japan positive list - by name and version, not just a generic phrase like "pesticide-free."

**Packaging and labeling specification.** Inner and outer packaging materials, label content requirements including batch number format, country of origin statement, and any destination-market mandatory labeling requirements.

This specification is attached as a numbered appendix to the quality agreement and referenced in every purchase order. Both parties sign the specification document. Any change to any element of the specification requires a written amendment to the quality agreement, signed by both parties, before it takes effect.

## Change Notification Clauses

The change notification clause is the mechanism that prevents the specification lock from being meaningless. It requires the supplier to inform the buyer in writing before making any change that could affect the product the buyer is receiving.

A well-drafted change notification clause covers:

- **Raw material source change:** If the supplier switches from one Indian growing region to another, or from one raw material supplier to another, the buyer must be notified before the first batch using the new source is manufactured. Growing region affects pesticide residue profile, heavy metal profile, and active compound concentration.

- **Extraction or processing change:** Any change to extraction solvent, extraction method, drying method, or particle size reduction method that could affect the chemical profile or physical characteristics of the product.

- **Production site change:** If the product will be manufactured at a different facility than the one audited and approved by the buyer, this requires notification and, typically, a qualification step for the new site.

- **Certification change:** Any addition, lapse, suspension, or withdrawal of a certification the buyer relies on - organic certification, Halal certification, GMP certification - must be notified immediately.

- **Subcontractor change:** If any step in the manufacturing process - extraction, spray drying, milling, packaging - is subcontracted to a third party, any change to the subcontractor requires notification.

The clause should specify a minimum notification lead time before the change takes effect - 60 days is a reasonable standard for most changes - and should require the supplier to provide supporting documentation confirming that the change will not affect the agreed specification. The buyer should retain the right to require a new sample batch from the modified process before approving the change.

## Batch Documentation Requirements

A quality agreement should define, with precision, what batch documentation the supplier must provide with every shipment. "COA and relevant certificates" is not a specification - it is an invitation to argument.

A well-structured batch documentation schedule specifies:

**Certificate of analysis (COA).** The COA must reference the specific batch number, the product specification version it is testing against, the test methods used for each parameter, the test results for each parameter, the acceptance limits, and a pass or fail conclusion for each parameter. It must be issued by a named quality manager at the supplier's facility or by a named third-party laboratory, with the laboratory's accreditation details.

**Third-party testing.** For regulated markets, the quality agreement should specify which parameters are tested in-house by the supplier and which require independent third-party laboratory testing. Heavy metals, pesticide residues, and microbiological testing to EU, US, or Japan limits typically require accredited third-party laboratory testing to be accepted by regulatory bodies and certification holders.

**Country of origin documentation.** A Certificate of Origin issued by the appropriate Indian authority (typically the Export Inspection Council or a designated Chamber of Commerce) for EU shipments, or a self-declaration of origin in the format required by the applicable trade agreement.

**Phytosanitary certificate.** Issued by the Indian Plant Quarantine authority for botanical raw materials and powders.

**Market-specific certificates.** Halal certificate for UAE/GCC shipments. Organic certificate and transaction certificate for EU Organic or USDA Organic claims. US FDA Prior Notice reference number for USA shipments.

Specify that documentation must be provided in English (or the destination-market language where required) and must arrive with the shipment or within a defined number of business days before the shipment's estimated arrival at the destination port.

## Non-Conformance and Dispute Resolution

The non-conformance section of a quality agreement defines what happens when the buyer receives product that does not meet the agreed specification. Without a defined process, non-conformance becomes a dispute, and disputes with Indian suppliers across international jurisdictions are expensive and slow.

A practical non-conformance process has three stages:

**Stage 1 - Non-conformance report.** The buyer issues a formal non-conformance report to the supplier within a defined window after receipt of the product - typically five to ten business days for physical defects, and within ten business days of receiving third-party test results for specification failures. The report specifies the parameter that failed, the test result, the acceptance limit, and the test method used. The supplier is given a defined response window - typically ten to fourteen business days - to acknowledge the non-conformance and provide an initial root-cause assessment.

**Stage 2 - Corrective action.** Within 30 days of acknowledging the non-conformance, the supplier provides a written corrective action and preventive action (CAPA) report identifying the root cause, the corrective action taken, and the preventive measures implemented to prevent recurrence. The buyer reviews and approves the CAPA before the non-conforming batch is resolved through replacement, credit, or agreed rework.

**Stage 3 - Dispute escalation.** If the supplier disputes the buyer's test results or the applicability of the specification, the quality agreement should specify an agreed third-party laboratory for retesting, with the costs allocated based on the outcome: if the retest confirms non-conformance, the supplier bears the cost; if the retest confirms conformance, the buyer bears the cost.

The buyer's rights on confirmed non-conforming product should be stated explicitly: the right to reject and return at the supplier's cost, the right to receive replacement product within a defined timeframe, or the right to a credit note for the non-conforming quantity. The quality agreement should not leave the remedy to negotiation at the time of the failure.

## IP and Confidentiality for Custom Specifications

When a buyer develops a custom specification for a herbal ingredient - a proprietary extraction ratio, a unique marker compound target, a formulation-specific particle size distribution, or a custom blend - that specification constitutes the buyer's intellectual property. It reflects their formulation knowledge, their regulatory strategy, or their product differentiation.

The default position under Indian contract law does not automatically protect a buyer's custom specification from use or disclosure by the supplier. A quality agreement must explicitly address this.

The confidentiality clause should cover:

**Non-disclosure.** The supplier must not share the buyer's specification, or any information about the buyer's formulation requirements, with any third party - including other customers, distributors, or the supplier's own parent company.

**Non-compete on specification.** The supplier must not manufacture a product to the same specification for any other buyer without the buyer's written consent. This is particularly important for custom extraction ratios or unique standardisation profiles where the specification itself is the differentiating asset.

**Marketing restriction.** The supplier must not reference the buyer's name, logo, or product in any marketing, promotional, or trade fair material without prior written consent.

**Term of obligation.** The confidentiality obligation should survive the termination of the supply agreement by a defined period - three to five years is a reasonable standard in the herbal ingredient sector, reflecting the time required for a competitor to independently develop and commercialise a similar product.

The confidentiality clause should also specify what information is excluded from protection: information that is genuinely in the public domain, information that the supplier can demonstrate they had independently before the relationship began, and information that the buyer discloses publicly themselves.

## Regulatory Compliance Obligations

For buyers supplying regulated markets - EU, UK, USA, Japan, Australia - the quality agreement should contain an explicit regulatory compliance clause that defines the supplier's obligations in relation to destination-market regulations.

This clause should specify:

**Compliance maintenance.** The supplier warrants that the product, as manufactured and documented, meets the regulatory requirements of the agreed destination markets. If a regulatory requirement changes in a destination market that affects the product's compliance status, the supplier must notify the buyer within a defined timeframe and work with the buyer to address the change.

**Regulatory change notification.** If the Indian regulatory environment changes in a way that affects the product - a change to FSSAI export requirements, a change to India's AYUSH export documentation requirements, a change to phytosanitary documentation requirements - the supplier must notify the buyer before the change takes effect.

**Regulatory support.** The supplier agrees to provide such documentation as the buyer reasonably requires for regulatory submissions, including facility information, process descriptions, and supporting certificates, within a defined response timeframe.

For EU buyers specifically, the quality agreement should address the supplier's obligations in relation to the EU Organic regulation if organic certification is a requirement, including the obligation to maintain unbroken certificate chains and to provide transaction certificates for each organic batch.

## Review Schedule and Version Control

A quality agreement is a living document. Regulatory requirements change. The buyer's formulation may evolve. Market-specific compliance obligations may be updated. A quality agreement without a review schedule is a document that becomes outdated and misaligned with the actual supply arrangement it is meant to govern.

Build in a mandatory annual review, conducted within 60 days of the agreement's anniversary date. The review should cover any regulatory changes in the destination market that affect the specification or documentation requirements, any product performance data from the preceding year that suggests a specification parameter should be tightened or adjusted, and any change in the supplier's manufacturing environment or certification status.

Each version of the quality agreement should carry a version number and an effective date. When an amendment is agreed, a revised appendix or a formal amendment document is signed by both parties and attached to the agreement. Earlier versions should be retained as part of the supply chain audit record - regulatory inspectors in the EU and USA sometimes require evidence of the specification that was in force at the time a specific batch was manufactured, not the current specification.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a quality agreement for herbal ingredient supply and how is it different from a purchase contract?**

A purchase contract defines the commercial terms of a transaction - price, volume, delivery timeline, and payment terms. A quality agreement is a separate document that defines the technical and quality standards the supplier must meet across all transactions under the commercial relationship. A quality agreement specifies the product specification and test methods, the documentation the supplier must provide with every batch, the procedures for handling non-conforming product, the supplier's obligation to notify the buyer of any changes to materials or processes, and the IP and confidentiality protections that apply to custom specifications. In a well-structured supply relationship, the purchase contract governs what is bought and at what price; the quality agreement governs how it must be made, documented, and delivered.

**What should a product specification lock include for Indian herbal ingredients?**

A product specification lock for an Indian herbal ingredient should define the ingredient's identity including botanical name, plant part, extraction solvent if applicable, and product form. It should specify the active marker compound and its target concentration range with the test method to be used, the physical parameters such as particle size and moisture content, the microbiological limits covering total plate count, yeast, mould, E. coli, and Salmonella, the heavy metal limits for lead, arsenic, cadmium, and mercury, and the pesticide residue standard applicable to the destination market. The specification should reference the exact test methods - AOAC, HPLC, ICP-MS - so there is no ambiguity about how conformance will be assessed.

**What is a change notification clause and why does it matter in herbal ingredient supply agreements?**

A change notification clause requires the supplier to inform the buyer in writing before making any change to the raw material source, growing region, extraction method, solvent, production site, or certification status for a product the buyer is purchasing. It matters because changes that appear minor from the supplier's perspective can have significant downstream consequences for the buyer - a change in growing region can alter the pesticide residue profile of a botanical powder, a change in extraction solvent can affect residual solvent content, and a change in production site can invalidate a facility-specific organic certification that the buyer's own regulatory filing references. Without a change notification clause, a buyer can receive technically different product from what they qualified, with no forewarning.

**How should IP and confidentiality for custom herbal ingredient specifications be handled in a supply agreement?**

When a buyer develops a custom specification for a herbal ingredient - a proprietary extraction ratio, a unique standardisation profile, or a formulation-specific particle size - that specification constitutes the buyer's intellectual property. A quality agreement should include a confidentiality clause that prevents the supplier from sharing the specification with any third party, from manufacturing the same specification for any other buyer without written consent, and from using knowledge of the buyer's specification in marketing or promotional material. The clause should define the term of the confidentiality obligation, which should typically extend beyond the commercial relationship and survive termination of the supply agreement by three to five years.

**How does dispute resolution work under a quality agreement with an Indian herbal ingredient supplier?**

A quality agreement should set out a structured three-stage dispute resolution process. In the first stage, the buyer raises a non-conformance report with supporting test data, and the supplier is given a defined response window - typically ten to fourteen business days - to provide root-cause analysis and a corrective action plan. In the second stage, if the response is unsatisfactory or no response is received, a defined senior contact at each party convenes to resolve the dispute within thirty days. In the third stage, if resolution at that level fails, the parties commit to independent arbitration under defined rules - the ICC arbitration rules are commonly referenced in India-international trade disputes - in a mutually agreed neutral jurisdiction. Embedding this structure in the quality agreement in advance reduces the cost and ambiguity of dispute resolution when it becomes necessary.
