A vague RFQ does not save time - it costs time. An RFQ that says "quote me ashwagandha extract, 1 MT" will generate a quote, but that quote will be based on whatever standardization, testing scope, and Incoterm the supplier defaults to, which frequently does not match what the buyer actually needs. The result is a second round of clarifying questions, a revised quote, and a sourcing timeline that took two rounds to do what one round could have done.

This guide provides a field-by-field RFQ template for herbal and botanical ingredient sourcing from India, explains why each field matters to the resulting quote, and gives buyers a checklist to confirm an RFQ is complete before it goes out. It is a companion to our guides on [herbal ingredient pricing and cost drivers](/blog/herbal-ingredient-pricing-cost-drivers-india-sourcing/) and [Incoterms and shipping logistics](/blog/incoterms-shipping-logistics-indian-herbal-ingredient-buyers/), both of which explain the underlying variables this template asks buyers to specify upfront.

## Why RFQ Precision Determines Quote Quality

An Indian herbal ingredient supplier receiving an underspecified RFQ has two options: ask a round of clarifying questions before quoting, which adds days to the process, or quote against their own default assumptions, which produces a quote that may not reflect what the buyer actually needs and has to be revised once the gap is discovered. Neither outcome is what the buyer wants, and both are avoidable with a complete RFQ.

The variables that most commonly go unspecified - and that most commonly cause quote revisions - are the ingredient's standardization or assay specification, the destination country (which determines the applicable regulatory testing scope), the Incoterm, and the documentation the buyer requires with the shipment. Each of these has a direct, and often substantial, effect on price. A buyer who specifies all of them upfront gets quotes that are both more accurate on the first pass and directly comparable across multiple suppliers.

## The RFQ Template: Field by Field

### 1. Ingredient Identification

- **Common name and botanical name** (with authority, where relevant) - e.g., Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera (L.) Dunal)
- **Plant part** - root, leaf, aerial parts, seed, bark, rhizome
- **Form** - powder, standardized extract, essential oil, oleoresin
- **Standardization or assay specification** - the specific marker compound and percentage (e.g., withanolides NLT 5%, curcuminoids NLT 95%, boswellic acids NLT 65%). If sourcing a non-standardized botanical powder, state that explicitly rather than leaving it blank.
- **Extraction method or solvent**, if relevant to your formulation requirements (water-extracted, ethanol-extracted, or a specific ratio such as 10:1)
- **Particle size or mesh**, for powders where this affects your manufacturing process

### 2. Quantity and Timeline

- **Quantity per order**, with unit of measure (kg, MT)
- **Order frequency** - one-time trial order, or an indication of anticipated recurring volume, since suppliers often price differently for a trial quantity versus an anticipated ongoing relationship
- **Target delivery date or lead time requirement**
- **MOQ acknowledgment** - state your target quantity even if you expect it may be below the supplier's stated MOQ, so the supplier can advise whether it is workable or propose alternatives

### 3. Quality and Compliance Requirements

- **Destination country and, where relevant, the specific regulatory framework applicable** (EU pesticide MRLs under EC 396/2005, US USP `<2232>` elemental limits, etc.) - stating the destination market is often sufficient for an experienced supplier to infer the applicable testing scope, but naming the specific standard removes ambiguity
- **Required certifications** - organic (NPOP, and USDA NOP if separately required), Halal (and the specific certifying body if your market requires ESMA recognition or equivalent), Kosher, GMP tier (WHO-GMP minimum, EU-GMP if pharmaceutical-grade)
- **Testing panel required** - identity confirmation method, heavy metals, microbial limits, pesticide residue screening, aflatoxins where applicable
- **NABL accreditation requirement** for the testing laboratory, and whether third-party (not in-house) testing is required

### 4. Documentation Required

- Certificate of Analysis (batch-specific)
- MSDS / SDS (GHS-compliant, current)
- Phytosanitary certificate
- Certificate of Origin (specify if preferential origin documentation is needed for a trade agreement such as India-UAE CEPA)
- Organic Transaction Certificate, if applicable
- Halal certificate, if applicable
- Any additional destination-market-specific declarations (heat treatment, irradiation status, etc.)

### 5. Commercial Terms

- **Requested Incoterm(s)** - request EXW and one shipped Incoterm (FOB or CIF) so ex-factory and landed cost can both be evaluated
- **Destination port or airport**
- **Payment terms preference**, if you have a standing requirement (advance payment percentage, letter of credit, etc.)
- **Quote validity period requested** - herbal ingredient commodity pricing can move with harvest seasons and currency fluctuation, so ask suppliers to state how long their quoted price remains valid
- **Sample availability and cost** - request whether a pre-order sample is available and at what cost or lead time

### 6. Response Requirements

- **Quote deadline** - a stated response date keeps the sourcing timeline moving and signals a serious, time-bound process to the supplier
- **Preferred response format** - a structured quote covering unit price by quantity tier, lead time, MOQ, and payment terms is easier to compare across suppliers than a narrative email response

## Common RFQ Mistakes That Trigger Revision Rounds

**Omitting standardization level.** "Turmeric extract" without a stated curcuminoid percentage will be quoted against whatever the supplier's most common export specification is - often 95% curcuminoids, but not always, and a mismatch discovered after quoting means renegotiation.

**Leaving destination country unstated.** Testing scope and applicable regulatory limits vary enough between the EU, USA, Japan, and other markets that a supplier cannot quote an accurate testing cost without knowing where the shipment is headed.

**Requesting a single Incoterm without landed-cost context.** An EXW quote from one supplier and a CIF quote from another are not directly comparable without adding freight and insurance to the EXW figure - request both from each supplier, or normalize manually before comparing.

**Not stating the testing panel.** A supplier quoting without a specified testing panel may price based on a minimal domestic-market testing scope, which is materially cheaper than a full EU-compliant pesticide screen at 0.01 mg/kg LOQ sensitivity. The gap between these two testing scopes is a common source of post-quote price disputes.

**Sending inconsistent specifications to different suppliers.** If the RFQ is refined after the first supplier responds and the revised version is sent only to remaining suppliers, the resulting quotes are not comparable across the full shortlist. Finalize the specification once, then distribute uniformly.

**No stated response deadline.** Without a deadline, supplier response times vary widely and the sourcing timeline extends unpredictably. A stated deadline, even an approximate one, produces faster and more consistent turnaround.

## Summary

A complete RFQ specifies the exact ingredient form and standardization, the destination market and applicable testing scope, quantity and timeline, required certifications and documentation, and commercial terms including Incoterm and payment preference - sent identically to a pre-qualified shortlist of suppliers with a stated response deadline. Buyers who specify these variables upfront consistently get more accurate first-pass quotes, avoid revision rounds, and can compare supplier quotes on a like-for-like basis rather than reconciling different assumptions after the fact.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What information should an RFQ for herbal ingredients from India always include?**

The exact ingredient by common and botanical name, plant part, form, standardization specification, quantity, required certifications, destination country and port, Incoterm, delivery timeframe, and required documentation. Omitting standardization or destination country is the most common cause of quote revisions.

**Why do I get such different quotes from different Indian suppliers for what seems like the same ingredient?**

Usually because standardization level, extraction method, testing scope, Incoterm, or MOQ tier were left unspecified, so each supplier quoted against their own default assumptions rather than a shared specification.

**What Incoterm should I request in an RFQ if I am not sure which is appropriate?**

Request both EXW and a shipped Incoterm (FOB or CIF) so you can compare ex-factory pricing against landed cost. State the Incoterm explicitly rather than leaving it to the supplier's default.

**How much detail on quality testing should go into an RFQ versus being negotiated after a quote is received?**

Core requirements - standardization parameter, heavy metals, microbial limits, and pesticide screening appropriate to your destination market - belong in the RFQ itself, since they affect the quoted price. Finer details like specific lab selection can be negotiated after an initial quote.

**Should I send the same RFQ to multiple Indian suppliers at the same time?**

Yes. Sending an identical, complete RFQ to three to five pre-qualified suppliers simultaneously, with a stated deadline, is the most reliable way to generate comparable quotes.
